GUIDING

A Day in the Life
of a Maasai Guide

Follow the pace of the savannah through the eyes of someone who calls it home.

A great guide in the Maasai Mara reads far more than tyre tracks and paw prints. For someone raised here, the plains are a familiar text — and a day guiding is equal parts field craft, storytelling, and quiet cultural translation.

Before sunrise

The day starts early, in the cool dark before dawn. It is the best time to move: predators are still active, the light is soft, and the plains are quiet. A guide checks the vehicle, reads the previous night's signs, and thinks about where the animals are likely to be based on season, weather, and long experience.

Reading the land

What sets a local guide apart is generational ecological knowledge — an understanding of seasons, plants, water, bird calls, and animal behaviour absorbed over a lifetime rather than learned from a manual. A distant alarm call, a shift in the grass, the direction of a herd: all of it is information. This is knowledge that grew out of a pastoralist life spent closely observing the same landscape.

More than wildlife

Guiding here is also about people. A good guide bridges the space between visitors and a living community — explaining customs honestly, setting expectations respectfully, and making sure encounters feel like genuine exchange rather than spectacle. The goal is for guests to leave understanding a little more, not just carrying photographs.

As the light fades

Late afternoon brings a second window of activity, and then the slow return as the sky turns. The best days end not with a checklist of animals seen, but with a sense of having been let into the rhythm of a place — and the people who belong to it.
