The Adumu:
Story of the Jumping Dance

The famous leap is a display of strength — and a rite of passage.

The image is instantly recognisable: young Maasai men, straight-backed, leaping high from a circle of chanting warriors. The dance is called adumu — from the Maa verb meaning simply "to jump" — and behind the spectacle sits one of the most important moments in a Maasai man's life.

How the dance works

Warriors, or morans, form a circle and sustain a rhythmic, layered chant. One by one, dancers step into the centre and jump vertically as high as they can, keeping the body upright and the heels off the ground. The others raise the pitch of the song as the jumps climb higher. It is a test of stamina, agility, and control, and a way for young men to show their strength.

Part of becoming a man

The adumu is most closely tied to Eunoto, the ceremony that marks the transition of morans from junior warriors into senior warriors and, in time, elders. Eunoto is among the most celebrated events in Maasai life, involving days of singing, dancing, and ritual. The leap is symbolic as much as physical — a metaphor for rising into the responsibilities of adulthood.

A heritage recognised worldwide

The male rites of passage the dance belongs to — Enkipaata, Eunoto, and Olng'esherr — are inscribed on UNESCO's list of intangible cultural heritage, recognised as a system that carries knowledge and identity from one generation to the next.

Seeing it respectfully

Visitors are sometimes shown a version of the dance, and it can be a genuine, joyful welcome. Approached with respect — understanding what it means rather than treating it as a photo opportunity — it becomes a real window into Maasai life rather than a performance.
